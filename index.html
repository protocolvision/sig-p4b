Protocol vision

Our method is to look at an organization through its protocols. An org chart shows who has authority, a strategy shows intent, and a software stack shows tools. Protocols show what actually recurs: how work moves, how decisions get made, how data is found, checked, and trusted. Recurrence is the clue. When the same thing keeps going wrong, or keeps going surprisingly right, in the same place, there is a protocol underneath it.

Good protocols are hard to see. They fade into the background and produce things that don't happen: the error caught, the dispute that never started. So protocol vision is a trained kind of attention, and the group trains it on three tracks:

  • Readings sharpen it. A year-long syllabus that builds toward a thesis of the AI-native, post-platform organization. Primary sources, one per session, with an optional companion essay per topic.
  • Observations train it. These are the reps. Every session, each participant brings one business protocol they observed. Over the year that makes an inventory of about a hundred.
  • Case studies apply it. These are the heavy lifts. Every participant carries one case study through the year, written to a shared template. Water rate data and construction bids are the worked examples.

Our current focus is AI adoption. AI tools unsettle the protocols a business runs on, and that briefly makes them visible again. As agents join the work, coordination shifts toward swarms: people and agents reading and writing shared signals. That gives us a rare chance to see how a firm really works, and to help redesign it.

Where we come from

The group began in 2025 as one of the first three special interest groups at Summer of Protocols, alongside memory and formal theory. Our notes and essays appear in its magazine, Protocolized. The group is now a research group of the Protocol Institute, which charters groups on protocol themes and funds time-bound projects.

2027 Research Focus: AI Native Data Operations

AI adoption now has hundreds of reports, adoption models, and case studies. That creates a second problem: working out which ideas and evidence fit a specific organization and rest on a consistent operating philosophy. Our approach treats data operations protocols as the place where effort pays off most. We now see those protocols as a question of swarm coordination.

In an AI-native organization, much of the work is done by people working through AI, like centaurs, and by agents that communicate with other agents. They coordinate less through meetings and hierarchy, and more through what each of them emits and reads: logs, events, feeds, boards, notes, and signed records. That puts three things at the center of data operations:

  • Emission and discovery. What an organization emits, where, who can read it, and how long it stays current. In the water case, utilities publish rates but nothing makes them discoverable. In the construction case, bid results are emitted as scattered public records.
  • Evals and model operations. Measuring what agents produce against real outcomes, and versioning the models, prompts, and context they run on. A forecast that is scored against the public result improves; an unscored one doesn't.
  • AI-mediated interaction. When people work as centaurs and agents talk to agents, discretion has to be delegated explicitly, checked cheaply, and recorded where others can see it.

We have built a capability maturity model backed by case studies, published as the Durable AI Adoption guide, and tested it in two pilot engagements, one with a water utility and one in construction. In 2027 we aim to rebuild the model around swarm coordination, systematize the playbooks, field-test them in more domains, and publish validated methods and case studies as open source. We are looking for research sponsors, and for partners in specific domains who want to apply these methods through consulting engagements.

Research Questions

  • What is an AI-native, post-platform organization, and how does it differ from the scale, process-redesign, and platform paradigms that came before?
  • How do organizations, teams, and people coordinate through what they emit, and what changes when agents read and write those signals too?
  • What should an organization emit, to whom, and how quickly should it decay?
  • How do evals and model operations keep a swarm honest, and what should be versioned, measured, and against what?
  • When people work as centaurs and agents talk to agents, where does discretion sit, and who checks it?
