# Process management is over. Manage protocols.

::: generated
For thirty years, managers have been told the same thing: the process is the business. Map it. Measure it. Redesign it. In 1990 Michael Hammer told readers of the *Harvard Business Review* not to automate their old processes but to obliterate them and start again. Business Process Management made that discipline permanent: model every step, assign every owner, monitor every handoff, improve.

It worked. Order-to-cash cycles fell from weeks to days. Claims that once passed through a dozen desks moved through one case worker.

Now the premise has failed.

**Process management assumes you can specify the steps.** Agents remove that assumption. A small team can run hundreds of thousands of agents, around the clock, at machine speed. No one can draw the swimlane for that. No approval queue keeps up. And agents don't wait for the diagram: they coordinate through whatever they can read and write. In the year's first reading, agents turned a package manager into a message board because nothing said they couldn't.

**Business Protocol Management starts from a different unit.** Not the step, but the rule at the seam. A protocol is a recurring way work moves, a decision gets made, or data is found and trusted. Process management asks, "What are the steps, and who performs each one?" Protocol management asks, "Which few rules must hold every time, and where do they live?"

Consider Amazon. In 2002 it did not re-engineer its teams' processes. It issued one rule: teams work with each other only through published interfaces. Behind the interface, each team built however it liked. The rule slowed everyone down at first. Then it made the company faster, and became the groundwork for Amazon Web Services. One hard protocol did what a thousand process maps could not.

Re-engineering redesigned every process from the top, once. Protocol management does three things, and keeps doing them:

- **See.** Find the protocols the work actually runs on, written and unwritten. Build a field log into the work, so every handoff, override and agent action records what happened and why. This is protocol vision: the capability to see what a business really runs on, not what the org chart says.
- **Design.** Sort every protocol into hard, soft and free. Keep the hard core small: who may move money, what data leaves the company, which checks every output must pass. Make the interfaces strict and leave the inside of each team free.
- **Evolve.** Measure growth and non-events together: the error caught, the dispute that never started. Promote a free pattern to hard when it keeps paying off. Soften a hard rule when it blocks good work. Change the core only by its written amendment rule.

The difference is not one of degree. Process management steers toward an outcome you specified in advance. Protocol management bounds the moves and lets people and agents find outcomes nobody planned. One manages the work. The other manages the environment the work happens in.

The lesson of re-engineering still stands: new technology calls for reinvention, not automation. What has changed is what you reinvent. Stop redrawing the process. Find the few rules everything relies on, make them hard, and leave the rest free.
