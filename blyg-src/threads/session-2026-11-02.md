# Kickoff: what agency law cannot reach

*Session of 2 November 2026.* Participants: Sebastián Aristizábal, Ben, Brown Elk, Dylan Fitzgerald, Andre Comeau, ComplexSy, Nick, Jose Leal, Surya, PAtwater, Tom Guarriello, divesh, Rika Goldberg, rafa, Mike Casey, bunkerheadz1998, Matthew McDowell-Sweet.

::: generated
A business that hands work to an agent needs to know who answers when the work goes wrong. That was the problem under the first reading of the year. Seventeen people joined the two-hour kickoff. The first hour covered the plan. In the second we read Noam Kolt's "Governing AI Agents" in silence, then each gave one reaction. The paper applies agency law and principal–agent economics to AI agents, and states its own limit:

> Incentive design, monitoring, and enforcement might not be effective for governing AI agents that make uninterpretable decisions and operate at unprecedented speed and scale. (Kolt 2024)

## What the room observed

- **Enforcement has no address.** Mike Casey: "With these agents, they're ephemeral, and there can be infinity of them. And so how do you actually enforce against a specific agent?" The usual principal–agent problem assumes a person or a firm that exists in time and space. Enforcing against an agent is a different task from enforcing against the lab that built it.
- **Autonomy is claimed more often than it exists.** Nick, from crypto: "people say that they're separate when they're not actually separate." Separation there often works as a liability shield. Split an agent across ten machines and the same question returns: "what level of obfuscation do you need to actually call it autonomous?" He found the paper passes on it.
- **Delegates drift.** Rika Goldberg, from DAO governance: "you have a delegate who is supposed to represent a token holder, but oftentimes, they don't talk to one another." DAOs have a record of this failure. She asked which of their pitfalls the group can study instead of repeating.
- **The paper has been overtaken.** Dylan Fitzgerald: "This is eighteen months ago in academia, and it is happening to us right now." His case was security. Restricting agent behaviour to stop misuse also stops the researchers who need to get ahead of it. Governance, he said, feels overdue, needed now, and premature, all at once.

These four reactions point at one gap. The principal–agent frame assumes parties that can be found, counted and held to account. An agent can be copied, split and ended. Rafa added that the group has no agreed definition of an agent as a software object: across one coding session, is it one agent or many? A rule about a relationship needs to know what stands at each end of it.

## Frames members offered

- **Four dimensions of scale** (Matthew McDowell-Sweet). Tempo: how long one action takes. Span: how long a mandate lasts. Width: how many agents act in parallel. Depth: how many layers of delegation sit between the principal's intent and the agent that acts. People occupy the middle of each one, from seconds to hours and four to seven items in working memory. Law and economics were built for that middle. The edges are where he expects new protocols to form.
- **Five spaces in the paper** (Sebastián Aristizábal): mandates and consent, loyalty and conflicts, delegation chains, evidence and audit, shared environments. Each could anchor a later session.
- **Extension, not delegate** (Ben). "I prefer the kind of Doctor Octopus model of AI use, where I view it more as extensions of human judgment." His claim: human review can scale at roughly a logarithmic rate if you sample outputs, then distil pieces of judgement into small models or deterministic steps that a person can review. Context does the signalling: "the pheromone medium is the context that gets read by various AIs." He plans to write this up, and the claim is untested here.
- **A delegation threshold** (ComplexSy). He disputed the paper's assumption that efficiency gains from delegation are limited, since small gains accumulate. He proposed modelling the point "beyond which you get serious potential legal issues or complications."
- **Grounding** (Jose Leal). When anyone can write and fork protocols, "what's the means by which you can ground what a good protocol is, what a bad protocol is?" The question applies to people and to agents.
- **Liability** (Brown Elk). From risk and insurance, the first question is who holds agency and who pays, for an agent as for an autonomous vehicle.

## The working thesis, and what the reading adds

Rafa's framing in the first hour: "because agents won't ask for manager approval, the style of management that we're working towards actually looks quite different." Gates work on people, because people can be given a reason to stop at one. The year's hypothesis is that management moves from gates to guardrails that match the tempo of the work.

Our reading of the discussion, which nobody stated in these words: a guardrail has to attach to something. If the agent has no stable identity, the rule attaches either to the environment the agent acts in or to the person or firm that started it. Which of the two a business chooses is a protocol decision, and one to look for when watching.

## Not settled

1. What counts as one agent?
2. Who is liable when an agent on autopilot does harm: the person who started it, or the provider of the model?
3. Does the delegate frame or the extension frame describe how members work with agents today?
4. Should rules come now, or after practice settles?

## The first hour

Rafa recapped eighteen months of sessions: organizations as knots of tensions, non-events, tempo, standards and adoption, and protocol hardening. The year's aim is protocol vision, the capability to see the protocols a business runs on. The six themes on the [Sessions page](https://protocolsforbusiness.com/sessions/) are a proposal, and members can argue for or against any reading. There are three ways to take part: observe, protocol-watch, or carry a case study. Members who carry a case can turn it into paid work through the group's leads.

In the introductions, members named the protocols they want to understand: construction procurement, personal knowledge systems, inference for small firms, DAO governance, open social protocols, brands as protocols, accessibility as a path of least resistance, the line between hardening a way of working and opening it for others to build on, and the difference between product thinking and protocol thinking.
