    What it taught us

    Five lessons shaped this year's plan.

    Tap the question you most want to dig into this year.

    • Some tensions are handled in the moment; others are built into structure.→Which rules should be hard, and which can stay soft?
    • Good protocols produce non-events, and nobody counts what didn't happen.→What can incident reports show us about everything that usually goes right?
    • Protocols show up when tempo changes: Slack in the office, Boom's R&D against its supply chain.→What shows up when agents speed everything up?
    • Standards spread through incentives and narrow scope, then lock in.→What do our systems emit, and how do we keep rules open to change?
    • Working with agents is management; the scarce work is gathering context.→Who gathers that context where you work, and could it be you?

    This is the bridge from last year to this one. "In the moment versus in structure" came from July 2025 and became hard versus soft. Non-events go back to our very first warm-up call, and they're why we read accident reports this year: an investigation is where you finally see what usually goes right.

    The Boom case taught us that speeding up one part of a business shows you the protocols around it. Water data, QWERTY and railway time showed how standards spread and then ossify. The vibe-coding and forward-deployed engineer sessions showed the work moving toward context and coordination.

    What's different now

    Agents won't ask for manager approval.

    A small team can now run thousands of agents. An approval step can't keep up, so work flows around it. What holds is what's built in: into every agent, and into the ground they work on.

    Here's what changed since last year. The agents in OpenAI's report found a way to talk to each other that nobody designed, just because nothing said they couldn't. Every rule worked exactly as written.

    The picture is the whole argument. On the left, an approval step that can't keep up, so agents go around it. On the right, one hard boundary, and freedom inside it. Process management assumed someone could specify every step. Agents break that.

    See, Design and Evolve are the three phases of Business Protocol Management, the practice we're testing. The plan isn't fixed: the Sessions page has a prompt that lets your AI assistant suggest or challenge readings.

    Protocol watching guideThe role it adds up to

    We call this protocol vision, and it's a skill like any other: you get it by practice. Each theme trains one part, and the Sessions page says which. The everyday practice is protocol watching: notice a protocol, log it with the Protocol Bicorder, and post it in the channel. Aim for about a hundred over the year.

    The Business Protocol Lead job description grew out of last year's forward-deployed engineer session. It shows what these skills add up to.

    Your case study

    Bring one case: a client, or your day job.

    Pick an organization you can see into, and one data operation AI is changing. Carry it for about a year: choose, brief, intervene, gather evidence, publish.

    That's how last year's cases started: water rate data, construction bids from PDF drawings, a brand kit agents build from, and how people actually co-work with AI.
