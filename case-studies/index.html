Case studies

The case studies are the heavy lifts. Every participant carries one case study through the year, written to a shared template. Case studies run alongside the observations (the reps) and the readings (the perspectives). The cases below are our worked examples. We tested the AI Native Data Operations approach in two pilot engagements, in industries that have little else in common. What they share: the important data sits in documents written for people, not machines, and each document comes from a different author. Reading those documents turned out to be the easy part. The hard part was finding the right document and trusting what it said.

1. California water rate data

Partner
California Data Collaborative (CaDC), with UNC for benchmarking
Period
2026. Two tracks are published. The work is paused on purpose while we decide the next phase.
Data problem
Water rates are public, but they are spread across hundreds of utility websites, PDFs, and board agendas

California's water utilities each publish their own rate sheets, in their own formats and in their own places. Earlier efforts tried to get utilities to file data in a common standard. Our view was that LLMs can now read almost any rate sheet well enough. The problem that remains is discoverability: knowing each utility's authoritative rate-sheet URL and trusting that it is current.

The engagement split into two tracks:

  • Benchmarking: a rate-sheet extraction utility and a benchmark study that makes comparing peers cheap. The published report includes a working bill calculator for 91 water systems. It computes a bill from each utility's own published tiers.
  • Discoverability: a registry of each utility's canonical URL, an llms.txt pattern, and a way to verify that a source is current, all without imposing a data standard.

Principle: focus on observability, and let LLMs handle standardization that is "sufficiently useful".

2. Construction bids from PDF solicitations

Partner
A heavy-civil and underground-utility subcontractor (sewer rehabilitation, CIPP lining, chemical grouting). Anonymized.
Period
2026. MVP pipeline built. Automatic sourcing is next.
Data problem
Every public solicitation is a PDF with its own pay items, caps, and submission requirements. Pricing evidence is spread across public bid results.

A contractor wins or loses a hard-bid job during the estimate. Every bid costs several days of desk work: reading the specification, measuring quantities, building the cost basis, checking wage rules, and assembling the package. Most bids lose, and the firm pays for the work on each of them.

Solicitations differ, but the estimating process is the same every time. So the pipeline has two layers that are kept separate:

  • The spine (code): intake, the bid board, artifact schemas, the audit rule, eval gates, and version control of each bid.
  • The content (agents): this owner's requirements, the research this job needs, and the pricing judgment.

An agent writes a bid plan after reading the solicitation. Code validates the plan and runs the gates against it. A new solicitation can bring new caps, requirements, or deliverables without any change to the code. Intake pulls pay items, quantities, and caps from the PDF bid form. It handles descriptions that wrap across lines, watermarks, extra tables of as-needed items, and blank quantities.

The calibration loop

Outcome data is public. State DOT lettings publish bid tabulations, council agenda systems publish awards, and engineers post results. None of it needs a portal account. That makes it possible to produce a shadow forecast for every regional letting and score it against the actual result, without bidding the job. The first scored forecast came in well above the winning bid. It overturned two assumptions the estimate relied on: one about who can be low bidder on specialty lining work, and one about subcontractor margins setting a price floor.

A measured forecast gets more accurate over time. An unmeasured one doesn't.

The full case study is written to the shared template. Client-identifying details stay private.

3. A small one: the Protocol Institute brand kit

The PI brand kit is a quieter example. It is a written specification (tokens, rules, and voice) that agents use to build real pages, such as the Durable AI Adoption deck and the AI Kitcraft workshop page. Its build log records a familiar pattern. A spec without an implementation gets re-derived, with new bugs, every time. Claims that aren't checked go stale. And the problems were caught by the person reviewing, not by the kit. The fix was a shared component layer plus checks that can be run automatically. That is the same move as the construction pipeline's spine, at a much smaller scale.

Your case study

Pick an organization you can see into: your own, a client's, or one whose records are public. Pick a data operation in it that AI is changing, or should change. Then carry it through the year. Short check-ins happen at the end of each syllabus part.

  • Nov – DecChoose. One paragraph on the organization and where the work gets stuck.
  • Jan – FebBrief. One page covering the template's first three questions: context, bottleneck, and the protocols in place before.
  • Mar – MayIntervene. What becomes code, what is left to agents, and what stays with people. Decide what you will measure.
  • Jun – SepEvidence. Run it, measure it, and record where the maturity model was wrong.
  • Oct – NovPublish. The full write-up, released as open source with the group's thesis and the revised methodology.

To start, copy the template into case-studies/your-name/ and open a pull request. You can keep a client anonymous.

Shared template

Every case, including each participant's, answers the same questions in the same order. That makes the cases comparable and lets them serve as evidence for the model.

  1. Context: the industry, the organization's role, and what the data is for.
  2. Bottleneck: where the work was actually stuck, and what everyone assumed was the problem.
  3. Protocols before: how data was found, extracted, checked, and trusted.
  4. Intervention: what became code, what was left to agents, and what stayed with people.
  5. Evidence: what was measured and against what, including where the model was wrong.
  6. Maturity mapping: the level before and after, and which move made the difference.
  7. What transfers: the parts of the playbook another domain could reuse.

What the two cases have in common

  • Extraction is solved enough. In both cases, reading the document was the easy part.
  • Discovery and provenance are the protocols that matter. The value came from knowing where the authoritative source is and requiring every figure to have one.
  • Calibration needs public outcomes. Water bills can be checked against published tiers, and bid forecasts against published tabulations. A model you can't measure against something doesn't improve.
  • Code the spine, leave judgment to agents. Keeping the fixed structure apart from the per-job content is what lets a playbook carry over to a new job or domain.
