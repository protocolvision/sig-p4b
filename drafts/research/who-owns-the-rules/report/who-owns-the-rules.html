PROTOCOL VISION

ABSTRACT

AI agents now act under rules about what they may spend, see and say, and when a person must step in. We asked who finds, designs and changes those rules, and whether AI is reshaping firms the way DevOps did. We read 118,526 job postings, 1,167 company filings, 38 practitioner recordings, 19 laws and policies and documents from 12 vendors. The tests were fixed in advance, and blind coding was checked against hidden examples. Running agents is common, and existing staff do it. Designing agents’ limits is a minority duty whose one stable home is AI security review. Finding unwritten rules and deciding changes across functions barely appear. The law will require overseers, not rule owners, and vendors are setting the pace. Inside teams the work looks like DevOps; in hiring it looks like a scarce-skills boom. We recommend four steps: name an owner for each agent’s limits where agents already run; keep a register of agents and their limits that the firm controls; name overseers before 2027; and test the rest in a pilot and in field work in twenty firms.

Three hundred thousand duties sorted into eight stable bundles of work, and only one involves AI

We read job postings the way a labour economist reads tasks: not by title but by the duties listed. Models split each posting into duty statements, a sentence model placed every statement on a map by meaning, and a clustering step looked for bundles of duties that recur across employers. A bundle counted only if it reappeared, from the same rules, in a held-out half of the postings. The figure walks through it on real data.

Four kinds of people do agent work today, and none of them owns changes to the rules

Read across postings, speech, law and vendor documents, four kinds of people keep appearing where agents meet the business. Each reviews, runs, builds or oversees agents; none is described deciding when an agent’s rules should change. They come from different sources and are not a ranked typology.

The AI security reviewer

Security architects review AI systems and agents before and after deployment: the one stable AI bundle, at 18 employers, mostly in regulated finance, with rule design at 30–40% of its duties. Its stability rests on one posting, and it may be an existing security duty extended to AI rather than a new figure.

I2, I3; cluster 111; Merck, IQVIA, Thrivent REQ-47040.

The embedded agent operator

Existing staff inside a function take on running agents as part of their job. Six of the ten speakers who describe staffing did this; the posting-side counterpart is mostly technical jobs.

I6 (one coder per speaker), I1; M072, M021, M073.

The AI or agent builder

AI, machine-learning and automation titles appear at 81% of employers, ask for AI-specific experience (27 of 30) and name AI teams. They form no stable bundle of duties, and with no time series their growth is not shown.

I1, I3; cluster 112 did not replicate.

The designated overseer

From 2027 the law requires deployers of high-risk or consequential AI to name trained people with authority to oversee and override it. The law does not make them owners of the rules, and no posting for this figure was observed yet.

I4; EU AI Act Art 26(2), Colorado SB26-189.

Two other figures are talked about but do not work as roles. AI governance councils, guilds and central teams come up in 5 of 34 speakers’ accounts (one a general IT governance team) and in 9 of 2,216 filing passages; members hold them part-time, and none is shown deciding agent rules. The “business sponsor” is a permission label in a vendor’s identity product: it splits business accountability from technical control on screen, but nobody is hired into it.

Because no one owns rule changes yet, the first step is to name an owner where agents already run

The findings point to three moves a firm can make now, and one it should test before committing to.

Name an owner for each agent’s limits

Agents already run inside functions, and the only place rule work shows up as a stable duty is security review. So give each agent a named owner for its limits: what it may spend, see and say, and when it hands over to a person. Start with the security or governance reviewers who already assess AI, at senior practitioner level, with the staff who run the agent proposing changes. It is a duty added to existing roles, not a new executive post.

Follows from findings 1–3. Low confidence on where it sits: the security bundle rests largely on one posting.

Keep one register of agents and their limits, under the firm’s control

Vendors now ship agent registries, sponsor and owner roles, and rename their products within about three years. A register that lists every agent, its owner and its written limits across vendors keeps the rules with the firm when a vendor console changes. It is also the artefact an overseer needs.

Follows from finding 5. Low to moderate confidence: untested against simply using each vendor’s registry.

Name trained overseers before the 2027 deadlines

Colorado from January 2027 and the EU AI Act from December 2027 require deployers of consequential or high-risk AI to name people with the competence, training and authority to oversee and override it. Pick them from the owners above, and record their authority in the register.

Follows from finding 4. High confidence on what the law requires.

Test the rest with a small exception-review pilot

Finding unwritten rules and deciding changes that cross functions barely appear in public sources, so they are bets, not findings. Run a review of agent exceptions and overrides in one or two functions, log what it finds and how long each change takes, and judge watching, workshops and simulations, the training for protocol vision, against that log.

Low confidence. We build Business Protocol Management, so we treat every step that would favour it as a bet to test.

Two things not to do. Don’t hire one senior “protocol lead”: no such posting exists among 25,512, and lasting roles in the past did not start senior. And don’t let vendor consoles hold the only copy of an agent’s rules.
