Who owns the rules when agents join the business?

Companies are giving AI agents real work: answering customers, approving expenses, redlining contracts. Every agent acts under rules: what it may see, spend and say, and when a person must step in. We asked which human activities are changing as a result, which bundle into jobs, and whether finding and changing those rules is becoming anyone's job.

Summary of findings

  1. Running agents is the work most visibly forming into jobs. A bundle of running live service agents (writing what they must never answer and when they hand off, reviewing conversations, owning answer accuracy) was named a likely distinct role in every round after job postings entered the pool. Posted titles exist ("AI agent operations specialist") at two to three years' experience, with a senior version at five to eight. Across all clusters, though, agent work stays spread inside functions: no cluster holds more than 29% of it. Low to medium confidence
  2. Rule design for agents is thin, and it does not cluster into one role. Designing or changing the rules agents act under was the main activity in 13 of 298 records (40 counting secondary codes). Its largest share, 46%, sits in admitting and permissioning agents, short of our pre-set 60% test; counting secondary codes it spreads over eight clusters. None of the 13 records shows a person in a company doing the work: most are vendor descriptions or rules saying what firms should do. High confidence it doesn't cluster
  3. Finding unwritten rules, and deciding rule changes across functions, don't appear in the coded record. Neither coder found a single record where either was the main activity. The red team found likely examples that the study's wording flattened, such as a support bot that invented a company policy and a postmortem of an agent incident. Absence here says more about public sources and our definitions than about companies. High confidence about the sources; low about practice
  4. Products assume rule owners that firms may not have named. Agent platforms build in a business sponsor for each agent, and approval agents run on a written policy, but no record names who writes that policy. These are hypotheses for fieldwork, not roles. Low confidence
  5. Entry-level work is where activity shrinks fastest (tier-one service, lead qualification, transaction matching), while the new agent roles start at junior to mid level. Medium confidence

The clusters did not meet our pre-set stability test, and several follow the type of source more than the work. Treat every finding as provisional; page 5 sets out the red team's objections.

Page 2 · Research design

How the study worked

Our earlier work started from job titles and our own idea of "protocol work", which invites confirmation. This study starts from evidence of changing activities, lets independent agents group the ones that travel together, and only then asks where rule work falls. The design and decision rules were committed before any evidence was collected.

Controls against our own bias

Our vocabulary was bannedCollectors and clusterers never saw "protocol" or our other terms, and didn't open our files.
Activities, not rolesEach record is a verb phrase in the source's words; collectors made no role predictions.
Broad sources, with quotasFive source types; at most half about governance; at least ten about shrinking or moving work each.
Independent roundsFive clusterings on pools of 60 to 298 records, in a random source order drawn in advance.
Blind codingTwo coders labelled every record without seeing any clusters.
Rules fixed in advanceTests for "clusters together", "dispersed" and "stable" were written before the run; a red team argued the opposite.

Page 3 · What is changing

Agents do the work; people supervise; few touch the rules

Of the 298 records, 109 describe new activities and 79 work being automated. Only 19 say where work moved from one group of people to another, which limits what any study of public sources can say about ownership.

What this means for protocol vision and protocol engineering

The agent operations specialist sits between two histories. Like the social media manager, it is a junior-to-mid operating role with posted titles. Like the prompt engineer, it is named after a technology. The case studies suggest it lasts if it comes to own a number (one posting makes the holder accountable for answer accuracy) and folds if it stays a tool skill.

Protocol engineering looks set to be a duty, not a job. Vendor documents and regulations assign it to agent operations, identity and access, legal engineering and risk roles, much as data protection became a duty of existing staff. If that holds in practice, the likeliest path is a specialty within these roles, not a new title.

Protocol vision is not yet visible as work. No public source here describes anyone finding unwritten rules from overrides and workarounds, or deciding rule changes across functions. Either nobody does it, or it is tacit work that public documents don't describe. Only fieldwork can tell: override logs, approval histories and interviews in companies that run agents.

Red-team review. A separate agent argued against the results using the same files. Its strongest objections:

  • The clusters follow the sources. Regulated supervision is 24 of 24 regulation records; admitting agents is 15 of 17 vendor records. Items listed in one document were counted as bundled work.
  • No rule-design record shows a person doing the work. They are vendor statements, rules for what firms should do, or single secondary reports.
  • The coders were not independent enough. Both were the same model; 85 of their 298 notes match word for word.
  • The design could hardly find rule-finding work. The definition needed a stated purpose that summaries rarely give.

What survives, in its view: rule finding and rule decisions are absent from summary-grade public sources (high confidence about the sources, low about practice); rule design does not cluster, and the clustering is unstable (high); whether rule finding and rule design would share a cluster is untested, not refuted.

Limits and assumptions to pressure-test

  • Every record rests on search-engine summaries; no source page could be opened.
  • All agents share one model and may have seen our project instructions; independence is of context, not judgment.
  • The question points collectors at work around agents, so the evidence can't show what happens elsewhere.
  • The case studies come from our exploratory research, partly from memory; seniority at the start is the least certain column.
  • We build Business Protocol Management, so findings that rule work matters suit us. The main finding here runs against our earlier view.

Next run

Open primary documents and drop aggregator pages; sample job postings at random instead of searching for "agent"; add a source type of rule-change events (postmortems, audit findings, policy revisions); stop counting items in one document as bundled work; seed known cases of rule finding to test whether coders can see it; use a different model or a person as the second coder; and interview about ten operations leads who run agents.
