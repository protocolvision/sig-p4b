Who owns the rules when agents join the business?

Companies are giving AI agents real work: answering customers, approving expenses, redlining contracts. Every agent acts under rules: what it may see, spend and say, and when a person must step in. We asked which human activities change as a result, which bundle into jobs, and how companies will organise the work of finding, designing and changing those rules.

Three studies: an activity-first scan of 298 records about work with agents; a calibration of that method against past roles; and a comparison with the industry that most resembles where agent-run business is heading.

Summary of findings

  1. Today, running agents is the work forming into jobs; rule work is not. Posted titles exist for running live service agents. Designing the rules agents act under is a thin duty spread across functions, and finding unwritten rules or deciding rule changes across functions doesn't appear in public documents at all. Low to medium confidence
  2. That silence is expected, and weak evidence. Applied early to past roles, the same method would have missed brand management, the CISO and site reliability engineering, whose authority came from internal agreements that stayed private for years. Medium confidence
  3. Algorithmic trading and the electricity grid are the nearest industries, effectively tied. Both have software acting on its own at machine speed, under rules encoded in systems and set from outside. Hospitals, our first idea, ranked last. High for the pair; low for either alone
  4. In large regulated trading firms, rule work settled into three forms. A mid-level risk and control team monitors and holds the kill switch; the people who design and change algorithms are registered or certified; the CEO or a named senior manager answers for the controls. The grid instead wrote its rules through industry committees, and smaller organisations let individuals own rule changes. Medium confidence
  5. The kind of outside pressure shapes the structure. Law produced named persons and certification; industry bodies produced committees and standards; where no pressure applied, as with retail pricing engines, no structure formed. Our pre-registered claim that structures form only after a law did not hold. Medium confidence

All evidence comes from search-engine summaries; no source page could be opened. Two red-team reviews argued against each study; their objections are on page 5.

Page 2 · How we studied it

Three studies, each designed against our own bias

Our early work started from job titles and our own idea of "protocol work", which invites confirmation. Each study below fixed its questions, rules and thresholds in the repository before collecting evidence.

1. Activity loop298 activity records from five source types; five independent clustering rounds; two blind coders labelling rule finding, rule design, rule decisions and agent operations.
2. CalibrationWould the same method have spotted past roles early? We reconstructed its verdicts for eleven roles, from the brand man (1931) to the prompt engineer (2022).
3. Industry analogueTen industries scored on eight weighted dimensions by two independent scorers from cited fact sheets; dated timelines of rule work for the top two and a control.
Red teamsA separate agent argued against studies 1 and 3 using the same files; a counter-example search tested the industry claims.
Page 3 · What is changing in software

Agents do the work; people supervise; few touch the rules

Two coders agreed on 291 of 298 records. Supervising and configuring agents is seven times as common as designing the rules agents act under, and neither coder found any record whose main activity was finding unwritten rules or deciding rule changes across functions.

The final round grouped the records into 14 bundles of work. Each dot is one record; blue marks rule design as the main (filled) or a secondary (ring) activity.

Running live service agents was named a likely distinct role in four of five rounds; building agents inside one function (legal engineer, GTM engineer) in four; admitting and permissioning agents in one. The clustering failed our pre-set stability test, and several bundles follow their source type, so these are provisional.

What this means for protocol vision and protocol engineering

Protocol engineering becomes a certified specialty, not a job title. Trading registered the people who design and change algorithms, at mid level, inside existing desks. Today's agent evidence already shows rule design as a duty inside service, legal, sales and IT roles.

Protocol vision becomes the inventory, then monitoring. When trading's stakes rose, regulators required firms to list every algorithm, its limits and its operator, and to define a material change. Finding unwritten rules is the work of building that list; keeping it true is the work of a risk and control team.

In large firms, cross-functional rule decisions sit with a named executive or a committee. A protocol lead there prepares these decisions rather than owning them. Smaller and community organisations let individuals own them.

Outside regulated finance, expect duties until outside rules arrive. The candidates are rules from payment networks and platforms that agents act through, a costly agent failure, and the EU AI Act's oversight duties (dates differ between sources).

Red-team reviews. Study 1: clusters followed their source types; no rule-design record showed a person doing the work; the coders were the same model; the clustering was unstable. Study 3: trading and the grid are effectively tied, and with the grid as the analogue the "only after a law" claim fails; the three-forms claim fails its own test, since individuals own cross-functional rule changes in some organisations; "incidents set the clock" was added after the fact; and trading has features agent-run business lacks: one unit of action (an order), a closed population of registered firms, and exchanges as chokepoints. What survives: the pair as the nearest analogue; finance regulators reusing their templates (an inventory, a defined material change, a kill function, a named senior owner) for agents; and pressure shaping form.

Limits and assumptions to pressure-test

  • Every fact rests on search-engine summaries; quotes, dates and figures need checking against originals.
  • All agents share one model and its knowledge of how past roles and industries turned out.
  • Trading's automated actions are uniform and measured in money; agents act across many kinds of work.
  • We chose the industry dimensions and weights; a different weighting could change the ranking.
  • We build Business Protocol Management, so findings that rule work matters suit us. The main findings here run against our earlier view of a single protocol role.
